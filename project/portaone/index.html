PortaOne: Cloud PBX Customer Portal

PortaOne’s Cloud PBX gives businesses a complete phone system in the cloud. I designed the customer portal where company admins run it themselves: extensions, call routing, numbers, trunks, live call-centre monitoring and billing, in one place.

Product Design
UX & Information Architecture
UI Design on Material Design

The Challenge

A business phone system is deeply technical: ring strategies, IVR menus, SIP trunks, dialing codes, call queues and emergency locations. Traditionally these settings live with telecom engineers, so every change means a support ticket and a wait.

The portal had to put that power in the hands of office managers and team leads, people who know how their company should answer calls but not how a PBX works, without hiding anything an expert still needs.

Optimizing the Experience

I organised more than a dozen tools into groups that match how admins think: the phone system itself, calls, trunks, physical inventory and the company account. The same patterns repeat everywhere: a searchable list, a detail page with tabs, and inline help on every technical field.

Built on Material Design, the portal pairs a calm blue navigation with a single orange accent reserved for primary actions and the active tab, so the next step is always obvious.

Five groups in a collapsible sidebar keep every tool within two clicks. For call-centre teams, a separate supervisor mode adds the Wallboard and Supervisor panel without cluttering the admin view.

Overview

The first screen answers “is everything working?” before an admin goes looking.

Three donut cards show the health of the system at a glance: registered extensions, assigned DID numbers and devices in use. A calls chart compares now, today and this week, licensing bars show how many seats are used, and an Add new panel puts the six most common setup tasks one click away.

Call routing

How a company answers its calls is the heart of a PBX, and the hardest part to explain. These screens make routing visible.

IVR menus are drawn as a live flow instead of nested forms. An incoming call branches by on-hours and off-hours, then by keypress to menus, transfers, DISA, voicemail-to-email or disconnect. Each node can be edited in place, and the orange plus adds the next step exactly where it belongs.

Every ring group shows its strategy as a coloured icon, live queued and active calls, and member availability. Hovering the member count opens a breakdown of who is available, logged out, in wrap-up or on a call, so supervisors can spot a staffing gap without opening the group.

Ring strategies are chosen from four cards that explain themselves: order, random, simultaneous or least used. Toggles reveal related settings only when they are needed, such as after-call wrap-up time, and inline validation flags a problem on the field itself before anything is saved.

Queue behaviour is grouped by the caller’s journey: entering the queue, what happens when it is full, and waiting in line. Admins set how many callers can hold, what plays when the limit is reached, and whether callers hear their position and estimated wait time.

Numbers & connectivity

Phone numbers and trunks connect the company to the outside world. The goal was to make their status and cost obvious.

Every phone number shows its country and area, what it is assigned to (an agent, an auto-attendant or an external number) and its monthly charge. Small icons flag special numbers, and filters by country and area make large number pools manageable.

Trunks are listed with their services, registration status and the device behind them, plus live incoming, outgoing and forwarded calls. A tooltip breaks down call limits, so admins can see capacity before customers hit a busy signal.

The trunk detail page uses the same tabbed pattern as extensions and auto-attendants. Credentials, display number and timezone come first, followed by the emergency location, so required safety information is never buried.

Configuration

Dense settings are unavoidable in a PBX. The work here was making long pages scannable and safe to change.

Dozens of star codes for parking, paging, recording, supervision and screening are grouped by feature, with a toggle to switch a whole feature off. Paired columns show the matching DTMF codes, and every code has inline help for admins who have never set one before.

An extension’s call settings stack voicemail, call barring, recording, on-hold music and call waiting as collapsible feature blocks. Each block opens only when switched on, small colour badges help tell features apart, and a sticky footer keeps Save in reach on a long page.

Monitoring

Call-centre supervisors need to see what is happening right now, not after the fact.

Recent calls show caller, destination, duration and charge, with a quality gauge that turns red when a call had problems. Date-time filters and an option to include unsuccessful attempts make troubleshooting a specific call quick.

A customisable wallboard for the office screen. Widgets show who is on call, ringing, idle or unregistered per ring group, with call stats per agent. Colour-coded status pills and timers like “On call 5 min 15 sec” are readable from across the room, and the board refreshes on its own.

Supervisors switch into a dedicated mode with team performance per ring group: logged-in time, average wrap-up, talk, hold and handle times, plus in, out and forwarded calls. Two rings in the header keep live agent and call counts always visible.

Billing

The last step of self-service is paying for it, without a call to accounts.

Billing shows balance and credit limit up front, with a clear warning when the spending limit is reached. Admins choose between card and bank account, and the billing address can reuse the company address with a single checkbox.

The Result

Company admins can now run their own phone system: build an IVR as a visual map, tune ring groups and queues, manage numbers and trunks, and pay their bill, all without a support ticket.

A small set of repeating patterns, a searchable list, a tabbed detail page and progressive toggles, carries across 14 very different tools, so learning one screen teaches the rest.
